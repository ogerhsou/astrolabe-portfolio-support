TRANSACTIONS · WORKED EXAMPLE

Keep stock trades and cash in sync.

A purchase moves cash into holdings. A sale moves the proceeds back. Following both sides makes your portfolio records easier to check.

A portfolio tracker can show the correct share count and still have the wrong total value if the related cash entry is missing. Think of each trade as a change in both holdings and cash.

Here is one sample ledger, from an opening balance through a purchase, a dividend, and a partial sale. All amounts are illustrative US dollars, and fees are omitted.

Start with $10,000 cash.

Your sample account begins with $10,000 cash and no shares. Total tracked value is $10,000. An opening deposit establishes the money available to invest; it is not an investment gain.

Buy 10 shares at $100.

The purchase costs $1,000. Cash falls to $9,000, and the ledger now holds 10 shares. At the same $100 price, those holdings are worth $1,000.

Total value remains $10,000. The purchase changed where your money sits. It did not create a $1,000 gain or remove $1,000 from your overall tracked value.

Common recording error: adding $1,000 of holdings while leaving cash at $10,000 produces an incorrect $11,000 total. Linking the trade to the cash account keeps both entries connected.

Receive a $20 cash dividend.

A $20 cash dividend increases cash to $9,020. You still own 10 shares. With the sample share price held at $100, holdings remain worth $1,000 and combined value becomes $10,020.

A cash dividend and a reinvestment are separate events. If your broker reinvests a dividend, the resulting purchase changes the share count as well. Record what actually happened rather than increasing shares for every dividend.

Sell 4 shares at $110.

Now suppose the sample market price is $110. Selling four shares produces $440 of proceeds, taking cash to $9,460. Six shares remain, worth $660 at that price.

Combined value is $10,120: $9,460 cash plus $660 holdings. The sale moves value between the two. The difference from the opening $10,000 reflects the dividend and price changes in this example.

What happened to cost basis and P&L?

In this single-purchase example, the four shares sold cost $400 and sold for $440, producing a $40 realized gain before fees. The six remaining shares cost $600 and are worth $660, producing a $60 unrealized gain. Together with the $20 cash dividend, that explains the $120 increase in value.

This example has one purchase and no additional deposits or withdrawals. More complicated histories require consistent records and careful treatment of costs and cash movements.
